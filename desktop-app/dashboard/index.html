1.

Integrated Academic Neighborhoods

Ross's modular curriculum requires highly collaborative, systems-based learning environments where students move fluidly between lectures, small groups, simulation, and independent study. The campus should be organized into academic neighborhoods that cluster lecture halls, active learning classrooms, anatomy labs, simulation spaces, and collaborative study areas around shared student commons.

Architectural planning implications

Cluster program elements to support fluid movement and peer collaboration; provide shared commons as social and academic hubs.

Typical program elements
  • Active learning classrooms
  • Small group rooms
  • Anatomy and simulation labs
  • Student study lounges
  • Faculty touchdown offices
Planning metric: ~ 80–110 ASF per student
2.

Study & Collaboration Commons

Given the intensity of the medical curriculum and the cohort-based learning model, informal collaboration spaces are critical to student success. The campus should provide distributed study environments that support peer-to-peer learning, exam preparation, and informal tutoring.

Architectural planning implications

Distribute study and collaboration spaces across the campus to support both group work and quiet study; co-locate with food and beverage where appropriate.

Typical program elements
  • Open study lounges
  • Group study rooms
  • Quiet study areas
  • 24-hour study zones
  • Coffee / food adjacent spaces
Planning metric: ~ 20–30 ASF per student
3.

Wellbeing + Resilience Spaces

Medical training environments require intentional spaces that support physical and mental wellbeing. The campus should include wellness-focused community spaces that help students maintain balance during an academically demanding program.

Architectural planning implications

Integrate wellness and counseling into visible, accessible locations; provide outdoor and contemplative spaces for resilience and retention.

Typical program elements
  • Fitness center
  • Outdoor recreation spaces
  • Meditation / reflection rooms
  • Student counseling and health services
  • Community lounges
Planning metric: ~ 15–25 ASF per student
4.

Residential Community

Because many Ross students relocate internationally, housing and residential life are central to building a strong campus culture. Student housing should integrate social spaces, study areas, and outdoor environments that reinforce community building outside of academic hours.

Architectural planning implications

Design residential precincts as neighborhoods with shared amenities and outdoor gathering; link to campus dining and academic zones.

Typical program elements
  • Student housing
  • Residential lounges
  • Shared kitchens
  • Outdoor gathering spaces
  • Campus dining
Planning metric: ~ 240–300 GSF per bed
5.

Campus Placemaking + Identity

The campus should reinforce Ross University's identity as a global medical education community. Placemaking strategies should support social interaction, informal learning, and institutional pride. Public spaces should function as the connective tissue between academic, residential, and recreational environments.

Architectural planning implications

Establish a clear hierarchy of outdoor and indoor public spaces; design gateways, quads, and plazas as memorable, multi-use settings for events and daily life.

Typical program elements
  • Campus quad / central green
  • Shaded outdoor study areas
  • Campus gateway
  • Event spaces
  • Student gathering plazas
Planning metric: ~ 20–25% of campus land area

We understand that the Doctor of Medicine program is structured as a four-year sequence combining foundational medical sciences education with clinical training. During the first phase of the program, students complete a five-semester, approximately 20-month Medical Sciences curriculum in Barbados. This pre-clinical phase follows an integrated, organ-system–based approach that combines anatomy, physiology, pathology, pharmacology, and behavioral sciences while incorporating early clinical exposure through simulations and community health experiences.

Following completion of the Medical Sciences curriculum and the USMLE Step 1 examination, students transition into the Clinical Sciences phase of the program. This portion of the curriculum occurs during years three and four and consists of supervised clinical rotations in affiliated teaching hospitals in the United States. Core clerkships provide direct patient care experience across major medical specialties, including internal medicine, surgery, pediatrics, family medicine, obstetrics and gynecology, and psychiatry.

The pre-clinical curriculum is organized into a sequence of ten integrated modules delivered across the first two years of study.
